Back when the line came from the newspaper, it felt carved in stone. Monday's paper said the Cowboys were -6, and that was that until you heard different on the radio Saturday night. The truth is the number was moving all week. You just couldn't see it.

Now you can. A betting line isn't a prediction; it's a price, and like the price of gas on the corner, it changes when something changes. Our morning report tracks every move from the first line we see.

Why lines move

  • News. A quarterback's ankle, a goalie swap, a star sitting out, a snowstorm rolling in. Remember the old snow games, when nobody could see the yard lines? Weather still moves totals.
  • Money. When one side gets more action than the books want, they nudge the price, especially if the money comes from bettors they respect.
  • Copying. Books watch each other like neighbors over the fence. When a respected one moves, the rest follow within minutes.

Key numbers

In the NFL, a lot of games land on exactly 3 or 7, a field goal or a touchdown. That's why a move from -2.5 to -3, or -6.5 to -7, matters more than -4.5 to -5. When a line crosses 3 or 7 in our report, the market has really changed its mind. Basketball and college football scores spread out more evenly, so they don't have the same magic numbers.

Words you'll hear

  • Opening line: the first number posted. Ours is the first line we record, which can be a bit after the true open.
  • Closing line: the last number before kickoff, often treated as the market's best guess.
  • Steam: a sudden move across many books at once.
  • Reverse line movement: the line moving against the side most people are on, usually a sign that bigger bets are on the other side.

A move is a story, not a tip

By the time a move shows up in the paper (or on this page), the new price already includes whatever caused it. Chasing moves doesn't beat the house's cut. We print them for the same reason the old sports page printed box scores: they tell you what happened, not what's going to happen.
