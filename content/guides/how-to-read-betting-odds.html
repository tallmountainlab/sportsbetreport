If you grew up before smartphones, you probably remember the column. Tucked in the back of the sports section, in type so small you had to squint, were the day's lines: a team, a number, another team. Your dad or your uncle would read it over coffee and say something like "the Packers are giving three," and you nodded like you knew what that meant.

Here's what it meant. Every game in our morning report still comes down to the same three numbers.

The point spread: who wins, and by how much

The spread is a head start for the underdog, the way you'd spot your little brother a few points in a driveway game. If the Chiefs are -3.5 against the Bills, the Chiefs have to win by 4 or more for a bet on them to cash. The Bills are +3.5: a bet on them wins if they win outright or lose by 3 or fewer.

The minus sign always marks the favorite. That ".5" is there so nobody can tie. On a whole number like -3, a 3-point game is a push and everybody gets their money back. "Giving three," in Uncle-speak, just means favored by three.

In our tables the spread column shows the favorite and their number, like Chiefs -3.5. "Pick'em" means it's a coin flip and nobody's favored.

The total: how many points in all

The total, or over/under, is a guess at the combined score. If it's 47.5, the over wins at 48 or more and the under wins at 47 or fewer. Think of every shootout you ever watched on a Sunday afternoon that blew right past the number in the third quarter. In hockey and baseball, it's goals or runs.

The moneyline: who wins, straight up

The moneyline forgets about the margin and asks the oldest question in sports: who wins? The price tells you how sure the market is.

MoneylineMeans$100 example
-150FavoriteRisk $150 to win $100
+130UnderdogRisk $100 to win $130
-110Standard price on a spread or totalRisk $110 to win $100

That gap between -110 and a fair even-money price is the house's cut, the vig or juice. We break it down in implied probability and the vig.
