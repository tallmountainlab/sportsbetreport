There was a time when the only way to know the line was to know a guy. Or to read the tiny column in the back of the sports section, or catch it on the radio. Here's how we got from there to a morning report on your phone.

A teacher's idea

For a long time, betting on football meant odds on who would win, which made lopsided games almost impossible to price. The point spread is usually credited to Charles K. McNeil, a Chicago math teacher turned bookmaker, in the 1940s. His idea was simple: instead of asking who wins, ask by how much. Suddenly every game, even a mismatch, was a fair fight on paper.

The Las Vegas years

Nevada allowed sports betting by 1949, and for decades Las Vegas was where the line was born. Small sports books, often called turf clubs, set numbers that the rest of the country quietly copied. The line traveled by phone and by word of mouth, and eventually into the newspaper, in that squinting little column of type.

The TV and radio era

By the 1970s and '80s the line had gone mainstream. Jimmy "the Greek" Snyder gave his picks on CBS's The NFL Today, and plenty of families argued about them over Sunday lunch. Late-night radio shows talked numbers, and newspapers ran the lines every week next to the standings and the box scores.

Everywhere at once

In May 2018 the US Supreme Court struck down the federal law that had kept sports betting mostly in Nevada. States began legalizing it one by one, and the line moved from the back page to the phone in your pocket. Sports betting is still illegal in some states, including Utah, so know the rules where you live.

What hasn't changed

The numbers are faster now, and there are more of them. But the ritual is the same one you remember: coffee, the morning paper, and a few minutes with the lines before the games. That's the feeling we're trying to bring back every morning. Read today's morning line.
